# Visual-joke punchline integrity

## Trigger

Use when conceiving, revising, evaluating or preparing memes, comic panels, humorous editorial illustrations, and visual breaks in long-form writing.

## Core rule

**Do not overcomplete the joke.** Build a single unmistakable visual situation with a strong incongruity, recognizable expression, or surprising reversal. The reader should grasp the action and emotional turn in roughly two or three seconds.

A caption, dialogue, label or title is valuable only when it **sets up a necessary premise, delivers the actual punchline, or adds a distinct comic beat**. Do not append a separate sentence explaining what the reader already sees. If the gag still works when an explanatory caption is removed, remove it. Trust the picture.

Avoid extraneous computer-message overlays or software-error callouts used to describe a non-technical human interaction. Use those devices only when the joke is genuinely about an interface and the owner wants that format. Do not turn visual humor into an infographic.

## Series-level judgment

- Use different mechanisms across successive illustrations: literalization, disproportionate scale, visual reversal, deadpan dialogue, tenderness, absurd animals, situational irony. Avoid repeating the same punchline under a changed costume.
- Keep compositions legible at article width. Exaggerate expressions and essential objects; omit unnecessary labels, signage, background jokes and visual clutter.
- Preserve the owner's revised dialogue and choice of scene. A note that replaces a joke is an **authoritative candidate revision**, not a loose suggestion to append underneath the rejected text.
- Where an emotionally serious passage warrants a visual break, use a touching, clear scene rather than inserting a joke into distress or emergency material.
- An image break belongs at an actual text wall; count existing media and prose blocks before adding another one.

## Admission check for each candidate

1. **Cold-view test:** Can the reader identify the situation, who is acting, and why the juxtaposition matters in two to three seconds?
2. **Caption-necessity test:** Remove proposed title/caption. If the visual action and dialogue still deliver the joke, leave it out. If the caption *is* the punchline or essential premise, retain it.
3. **Series distinctness test:** Is the joke materially different from other illustrations planned for the same piece?
4. **Owner-brief test:** Do accepted dialogue, the joke's target, safety constraints, and the requested comic style still match the owner's choices?
5. **Export test:** The selected concept and its exact placement anchor, including revisions, are saved together in the handoff.

If any test fails, revise the concept rather than adding explanation to compensate.

## Transfer provenance and limits

Origin: **owner feedback, 2026-11-07**, during review of a long illustrated article. The transferable failure mode was repeatedly explaining self-evident visual jokes with extra captions and returning to similar punchlines across a sequence. This is an extract of the correction, not a conversation transcript.

This rule is **not a ban on captions**. Some excellent comics depend on a written setup, caption-only punchline, or dialogue. The controlling question is whether each word has a real comedic or emotional job. Follow the current author's explicit preference where it differs.
