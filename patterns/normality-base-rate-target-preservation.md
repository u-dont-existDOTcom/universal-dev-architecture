# Normality and base-rate target preservation

## Purpose

Prevent a common reasoning substitution: answering whether something **can occur through an ordinary or nonpathological mechanism** when the actual question is whether the observation is **normal, common, typical, unusual, rare, frequent, or prevalent**.

Mechanistic plausibility and population frequency are different variables. A mechanism can be ordinary while a particular response magnitude, timing, threshold, selectivity, or presentation is uncommon.

## Activation

Apply whenever the user asks, directly or implicitly, whether an observation is normal, common, typical, usual, unusual, rare, frequent, prevalent, or what percentage of a population would show it.

## Core rule

Treat **population prevalence or distribution of the exact observation** as the target variable. Do not infer commonness from physiological plausibility, a known mechanism, nonpathological status, expert familiarity with the mechanism, or the fact that the response has been experimentally demonstrated.

For frequency questions, explaining **why X can happen** does not answer **how often X happens**.

## Procedure

1. Define the frequency target precisely: the phenomenon, population, conditions, magnitude, timing, threshold or dose, and relevant comparison state.
2. Preserve the highest-information qualifiers. Examples include **a lot**, **immediate**, **after only a small amount**, **only under X but not Y**, or **others exposed under similar conditions do not show it**. Do not normalize the full observation merely because a weaker version has an ordinary mechanism.
3. Prefer direct evidence that can estimate frequency or distribution: representative prevalence studies, cohort or survey frequencies, reference distributions/ranges, population comparisons, or trials with an appropriate sampling frame and measured event frequency.
4. Treat mechanistic studies, challenge experiments, case reports, and demonstrations that a response can occur as evidence of possibility or mechanism unless their design also supports a population-frequency estimate.
5. Keep three judgments separate:
   - **possible / mechanistically plausible**;
   - **nonpathological / potentially benign**;
   - **common / typical in the relevant population**.
   Evidence for one does not establish the others.
6. If direct frequency evidence is unavailable, say that prevalence or typicality is unknown. Any order-of-magnitude estimate must be labeled as inference and its basis stated.
7. Answer the frequency question first. Mechanistic explanation may follow, but it must not replace the base-rate answer.

## Interaction with other universal reasoning controls

- **Target preservation:** for normality questions, the held-fixed target is population frequency/distribution, not mechanism or pathology.
- **Evidence direction:** magnitude, timing, threshold, and selective contrasts must be predicted by any proposed explanation rather than discarded.
- **Specificity:** compare positive cases with tolerated/non-reactive controls rather than relying on features shared by all exposed cases.
- **Risk language:** denominator and population checks remain applicable to risk claims; this pattern generalizes the same discipline to all normality/commonness questions.

## Regression cases

### Hot beverage sweating

Observation: a person who was not previously sweating becomes conspicuously sweaty shortly after hot tea in warm weather.

Incorrect: `Hot liquids can activate thermoregulatory sweating, therefore this is normal/common.`

Correct: the mechanism supports **possibility**. To call the conspicuous response **common or typical**, look for frequency/distribution evidence for a comparable magnitude and context. If that evidence is unavailable, say so. An ordinary thermoregulatory pathway can still produce an unusually strong or low-threshold response in a minority of people.

### Benign variant

Incorrect: `This variant has a benign mechanism, therefore it is normal.`

Correct: benign/nonpathological and common are distinct. Estimate its population frequency before calling it typical.

### Experimental response

Incorrect: `Researchers reliably induced response X in a laboratory challenge, so response X is common in everyday life.`

Correct: the experiment establishes inducibility under its conditions. It estimates everyday prevalence only if its sampling and exposure conditions justify that inference.

## Completion check

Before answering a normality/commonness question, ask:

> Am I about to use evidence that X **can happen** to answer how often X **does happen** at the user's stated magnitude, timing, threshold, conditions, and population?

If yes, stop and obtain frequency/distribution evidence or state that the base rate is unknown.
