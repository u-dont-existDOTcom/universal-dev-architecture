# Consilience and expected observability

**Status:** REQUIRED OWNER RULE
**Origin:** OWNER, 2026-10-06

## Core rule

For a practical effectiveness claim, especially a large, surprising, rapid, or commercially actionable effect, do not let one study or one evidence family carry the conclusion when materially independent evidence streams are reasonably available. Test for consilience and expected observability before ranking the effect as established or practically important.

The larger, faster, more perceptible, and more widely exposed the claimed effect is, the more we should expect it to leave traces outside the originating study. When an exact or materially equivalent preparation is widely available and the outcome is salient to ordinary users, repeated absence of the claimed effect from independent firsthand reports is negative evidence. It does not by itself prove a null effect or misconduct, but it lowers confidence in the magnitude, generalizability, or credibility of the estimate until the discrepancy is explained.

## Activation

Use this rule when a recommendation or ranking depends on effectiveness; a small study set reports an unusually large effect; the intervention is commercially available enough for real-world observation; traditional use is invoked to support a modern endpoint; or formal studies and firsthand reports conflict.

Do not require review/forum evidence where users could not reasonably observe the outcome or the exact intervention is not accessible.

## Procedure

### 1. Freeze the exact claim

Record intervention/formulation, population, comparator, outcome and measurement, effect magnitude with units and uncertainty, duration, and major co-interventions. Prefer numerical effects and uncertainty to a binary p-value label.

Do not turn a broad historical concept such as vitality, recovery, nourishment, or strength into a modern endpoint such as a quantified 1-RM increase without evidence that the meanings match.

### 2. Seek semi-independent evidence streams

When available and decision-relevant, check:

1. Formal intervention evidence: independent replications, systematic reviews, preregistration, outcome validity, attrition, analysis choices, sponsor/investigator relationships, and null or negative trials.
2. Identity and mechanism: whether the material sold is the material studied and whether proposed mechanisms survive direct tests. Mechanism supports plausibility; it does not replace outcomes.
3. Historical/traditional evidence: what the material was actually used for and in what preparation. Use this as a qualitative prior or discovery signal, not a numeric effect estimate without matched outcomes and denominators.
4. Real-world firsthand evidence: for purchasable interventions with noticeable effects, inspect mixed and critical reviews, specialist forums, long-term reports, named-comparator experiences, and explicit no-effect reports.
5. Adjacent objective traces when they genuinely bear on the claim.

Marketing copy, retailer summaries, articles, and forum posts that all repeat the same trial count as one lineage, not independent confirmation.

### 3. Expected-observability test

Before treating real-world silence as evidence, ask whether the exact preparation is available, enough people are exposed, the claimed outcome would be noticeable, the reporting surface contains users who care about that outcome, and the effect should emerge within ordinary use.

When those conditions mostly hold, a large effect predicts a visible real-world trace. Search deliberately for both positive and negative reports. If a widely sold sports supplement truly adds a large amount of strength in weeks, strength-oriented buyers and lifting communities should repeatedly notice it.

When the conditions do not hold, classify silence as weak or uninformative.

### 4. Diagnose discordance

If evidence streams disagree, inspect formulation/dose identity, trained versus untrained populations, learning effects, direct versus predicted outcomes, diet/training/adherence control, attrition and baseline imbalance, analysis flexibility, sponsor concentration, publication bias, expectation and selection effects in reviews, responder heterogeneity, and real-world exposure.

Do not average discordance away. Narrow the conclusion to what converges.

## Decision rule

- **Convergent:** multiple reasonably independent streams support a compatible direction and magnitude.
- **Promising but discordant:** formal evidence suggests benefit while replication, field observation, historical use, or community experience is weak/conflicting. Do not rank by the largest point estimate; state the mismatch and use a conservative magnitude.
- **Isolated:** the claim rests mainly on one study, research group, sponsor-linked program, or correlated evidence lineage. Treat it as a lead, not an established practical effect.
- **Contradicted:** stronger independent evidence points the other way.

An unexplained mismatch may justify "likely inflated or non-generalizable" without establishing misconduct.

## Reviews and forums

When real-world evidence is relevant, verify current availability and formulation first; seek mixed, critical and no-effect reports as deliberately as positive ones; separate firsthand use from marketing repetition; preserve dose, duration, background, comparator and concurrent changes when stated; prefer domain-specific communities; and do not estimate incidence or causal effect size from selected reviews.

Reviews/forums are discrepancy detectors and heterogeneity evidence, not informal randomized trials.

## Regression cases

### Large study effect, popular exact product, little real-world trace

One small trial reports an extra 15-20 kg of strength after several weeks. The exact extract is widely sold to gym users. Large retailer corpora and lifting forums mostly report unrelated effects, while experienced users explicitly report no strength change. Do not rank by the trial point estimate. Investigate measurement, training status, sponsor dependence and replication; classify the strength effect as isolated or discordant until reconciled.

### Traditional strength label projected onto modern 1-RM

A traditional text calls a plant strengthening or nourishing. Modern marketing treats that as confirmation of a large barbell-strength effect. Reject the semantic jump.

### Sparse product with no reviews

A specialized extract has few buyers and no active user community. Absence of reports is not useful negative evidence; mark the stream underexposed.

### Many pages repeat one study

Retailers, blogs and press releases all repeat the same trial. Count the lineage once. Correlated repetition is not consilience.

## Transfer rationale and limits

This rule originated after a medicinal-plant strength ranking over-weighted large effects from a few trials despite weak real-world echo. It transfers to devices, software, therapies, supplements, training methods and other practical interventions.

The transferable principle is not that forums outrank trials. A large, observable, widely exposed practical effect makes predictions across partly independent evidence streams; unexplained failure of those predictions is decision-relevant negative evidence.

No fixed number of streams is required, anecdotes are not controlled causal estimates, and novel or inaccessible interventions are not penalized merely for lacking reviews.
