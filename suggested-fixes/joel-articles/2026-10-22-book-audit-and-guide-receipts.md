### B1 — Explain the connections among needs, schemas, coping and current states

Sources: Y chapter 1, especially pp. 7–8 and 30–42; Y chapter 8 pp. 271–278; G chapter 2 pp. 10–20; H chapters 1–4 and 11.

A schema is an enduring pattern of memories, emotions, bodily sensations and cognitions, not merely a literal inherited parent voice. Coping describes what happens in response; a mode is a current state/configuration, not necessarily a separate personality. The guide has inherited messages, guards, nonverbal experience and hooks but leaves their relationship underexplained.

Minimal placement: a bridge from *The Parent You Inherited* into *Two Common Protective Patterns*, with one ordinary example carried through rather than a new roster of inner characters. Identify trigger, old expectation, felt reaction, coping response, immediate function and later consequences. No mandatory recovered memory, childhood explanation for every habit or diagnosis from appearance. A treat can be pleasure, useful short-term regulation, persistent avoidance or impulsive gratification; its function is not visible from the act alone.

The older vocabulary is surrender/avoidance/overcompensation. G draws on the proposed resignation/avoidance/inversion reformulation. Keep the distinction in source notes or a small appendix note; plain verbs suffice in reader prose. Do not map either mechanically onto fight/flight/freeze. Do not present every criticism or unwanted thought as a distinct part.

### B2 — Explain how a pattern keeps the expectation believable

Sources: Y pp. 30–32 and chapter 5; H chapter 13 (couples/mode cycles).

The important extension beyond short-term relief is maintenance: discounting contrary evidence, avoiding situations where a different outcome might occur, and sometimes interactions in which each person's coping becomes the other's trigger. The guide already distinguishes experience from interpretation and discusses relational hooks, but does not put the feedback loop together clearly.

Continue B1's same example: expecting rejection, hiding distress, receiving little support because nobody knows, and interpreting that lack as proof nobody cares. Explicitly retain the alternative that other people really may be unavailable or harmful. No victim-blaming, equal-responsibility assumption or invitation to be vulnerable with someone coercive. A short callback under *Other Folks Also Have Hooks* can show a reciprocal cycle without importing a couples-treatment manual.

### B3 — Link existing adult functions to the coping behavior's function

Sources: Y chapters 5–6; G pp. 103–107 and 178–200; H chapters 4, 11, 14 and 17; C chapters 6–7.

The earlier answer overstated this gap. *The Inner Guide* already pairs care with limits; *Not Every Hero Wears A Cape* already teaches action planning, practical support, rehearsal and review; the daily-review practice already includes recognition, repair and progress. These functions are not missing.

Add only a connection: what need or feared outcome is the pattern trying to address, and what response meets that need without perpetuating the harmful strategy? Nurturer, Protector and Guide remain three functions of one developing adult. Explain that understanding the fear behind a punitive internal message does not require letting it continue humiliating the vulnerable self. Schema books sometimes reject/externalize punitive messages more strongly than the guide's guard-first approach; preserve this real difference rather than claiming the methods identical or demanding exile of a part.

### B4 — Prepare for setbacks and the ending/reduction of important support

Sources: G chapter 7, especially pp. 214–229; H chapter 20.

The guide covers daily review, repair, distributed support, grief and general life transitions. The less-developed point is advance preparation for a predictable trigger, plus the particular transition when a therapist or major support becomes less available. Healthy independence does not mean needing nobody.

Minimal placement: a forward-looking extension of the morning/evening practice (early cue, one rehearsed response, available support and how to resume help), plus a short note near *Borrowed Adulthood in Relationship* or *Finding a Therapist to Borrow Adulthood*. Discuss endings rather than interpreting them automatically as rejection or proof that earlier care was false. Some endings are premature or disappointing; do not assume successful graduation, prescribe fixed duration or promise permanent therapist access.
