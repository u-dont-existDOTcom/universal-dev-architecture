## 2. T1 — The names and categories we are carrying forward

### The problem these names solve

The current guide sometimes puts **Protector**, **protective part**, **inner parent**, and **the parent you inherited** close together. The concepts can be sound while the reader loses track of which function is meant. The particularly clear collision is in *Hear the Protective Part First*:

> Your Protector, the grown-up you, earns that protective part's trust about the same way, mostly out in ordinary life.

The intended relationship is between developing adult protection and an established guarding response. Using almost the same name for both makes that relationship unnecessarily difficult to follow. The earlier discussion proposed an **Adult Protector versus guard** distinction, then expanded the account to include **coping patterns** after Joel raised candy eating. Both steps belong in the final proposal. [D, terminology discussion and subsequent candy exchange.]

### The working vocabulary

| Preferred label | What it refers to | What it must not imply |
|---|---|---|
| **Inner Adult** | The developing capacity to care, evaluate, protect, guide and take responsibility in the present | A perfectly wise entity, the person’s chronological age, or an already-complete Higher Self |
| **Nurturer** | The Inner Adult’s warmth, receptivity, validation and care | A separate person who only comforts and never acknowledges consequences |
| **Adult Protector** | The Inner Adult’s protective function: boundaries, safety, stopping harm, practical protective choices | Every fear, defense or avoidance response, merely because it seems protective |
| **Guide** | The Inner Adult’s direction, discernment, learning, standards, realistic limits and longer-term orientation | An unquestionable authority, inner critic, or discipline that overrides care |
| **Little one / Inner Child** | The younger or childlike emotional experience, including vulnerability, anger, wanting, joy, creativity and play | Only a wounded victim, a permanently defective child, or an entity required to answer in words |
| **Inherited parent script** | Learned rules and ways of treating oneself; these can be helpful as well as harsh | Necessarily a literal voice, only the biological parents’ influence, or a separate inner adult |
| **Guards** | Protective reactions or recognizable guarding patterns, used as a metaphor when it fits the person’s experience | A confirmed protective purpose for every reaction, or an instruction to obey everything a guard proposes |
| **Coping patterns** | The broader description of learned ways of responding to distress or an activated painful expectation, including ordinary behavior with no distinct inner voice | A diagnosis, a catalogue of autonomous personalities, or a synonym for “bad habit” |

Use **reaction** or **response** when its function is not yet known. Do not decide in advance that anger, a yawn, numbness, refusal or an urge is a guard. The current guide already makes some of these distinctions; preserve them.

The important hierarchy is simple: **one Inner Adult, three jobs**. The little one, inherited scripts, and coping/guarding responses describe different aspects of experience—not extra adult functions. A **schema** and a **mode**, explained below, are explanatory terms at different levels, not two additional characters to introduce into the household.

### Guards and coping patterns are related, not competing names

A guard is useful language when someone experiences an identifiable protective stance: “Do not trust this,” “Stay unnoticed,” or a shutdown that seems to prevent exposure. A coping pattern may be recognizable only in what happens repeatedly: after criticism, the person scrolls for two hours; when worried about abandonment, they agree to things they do not want.

Not every coping pattern needs to be personified. Nor should every ordinary act of comfort be called guarding. **Coping patterns supplies the broader functional explanation; guards remains the guide’s accessible language for protective responses.** This distinction directly preserves the revision prompted by Joel’s candy question. [D, revised recommendation.]

The categories can overlap. A harsh self-critical message may repeat an inherited rule and also function as an attempt to prevent rejection. Those are two descriptions of one process; they do not establish two people inside. Equally, a child’s protest need not be relabelled a defense against some deeper feeling before it deserves attention.

### How this relates to the books

Young’s familiar mode framework distinguishes child modes, dysfunctional parent modes, maladaptive coping modes and the Healthy Adult. Capacchione distinguishes a healthy **Protective Parent** from the **Protector-Controller** discussed in Voice Dialogue. These are useful precedents for disambiguation, not proof that Joel’s categories are identical to either author’s entire system. [Y, ch. 8, pp. 271–278; C, ch. 6.]

Healthy adult functioning should not be defined as “always conscious and deliberate” while guards are “always automatic.” A practiced good response can become natural. The relevant contrast is flexibility, care, responsiveness to present evidence and capacity to revise—not merely whether the response feels fast or slow. This qualification is an editorial synthesis consistent with the guide’s existing account of adult capacities becoming natural; it is not a new test of clinical readiness. [A, *When Healing Turns Into Checking*; G, chs. 6–7.]

### Exact proposed operations

**Opening definition.** In *The Chicken-and-Egg Problem*, keep the existing paragraph beginning “That’s one grown-up doing three jobs” and its rent/party/bathroom example. It already explains the central relationship well. Add only a short forward clarification **after that complete paragraph, before “The Guide is the one people misunderstand the most.”** Do not duplicate the earlier proposed four-sentence primer wholesale.

Illustrative content to humanize later:

> I’ll call that grown-up your Inner Adult. The Adult Protector is one of its jobs; the guards we’ll meet later are the protective reactions you already learned. And the parent script you inherited is the way you learned to treat yourself, not necessarily the grown-up you want to become.

This example omits a full coping-pattern lecture at the opening; that explanation belongs later. Helpful inherited care remains useful, and the actual article wording must preserve that point.

**Formal label.** Propose “Adult Protector” where the adult function is named, especially at first definition and wherever it is contrasted with a guard. Plain verbs such as “protect yourself” need no replacement. Do not mechanically rename every occurrence of “protective.”

**Headings.** At the same heading levels, change:

- *Two Common Protective Patterns* → **Two Common Guarding Patterns**.
- *Hear the Protective Part First* → **Hear the Guard First**.

**Collision sentence.** Replace only the sentence quoted above with the previously proposed:

> Your Adult Protector earns that guard's trust about the same way, mostly out in ordinary life.

Review nearby “that part” references in context; some can remain natural anaphora. Update every reference to the two renamed headings, including earlier references in borrowed-adulthood material and later altered-state material. Keep the examples, uncertainty, willingness and stopping provisions.

**Inherited-parent heading.** Keep *The Parent You Inherited* as an evocative heading unless Joel chooses otherwise. Use **inherited parent script** in its explanatory prose to make clear what the metaphor means. A heading need not be turned into a clinical label to be unambiguous.

## 3. T2 — An inherited script is not the whole schema

### What needs explaining

The current inherited-parent section says how a learned critic can treat the person as never good enough, or label any improvement fake. This already does useful work. But the broader pattern can exist **without a sentence or speaker**: a delay in a reply may simply feel like abandonment; asking for help may feel wrong before the person can articulate a reason.

In Young’s account, a schema includes memories, emotions, bodily sensations and cognitions concerning oneself and relationships. It is not just a parental command. In van Genderen’s reader-facing language, a **pitfall** is a recurring pattern that can be activated in a difficult situation. These descriptions explain why a person can experience the pattern without hearing an inner voice. [Y, ch. 1, pp. 7–8; G, ch. 2.]

### Proposed reader-facing distinction

Keep three levels apart:

**A learned script:** a rule or way of treating oneself, such as “Do not bother people with your needs.” It may be experienced verbally or enacted without words.

**A broader expectation:** a recurring felt expectation such as “If people see that I need them, they will reject me.” It can include memory, bodily alarm and emotion, not merely a belief one consciously chooses. “Old expectation” is a useful plain-language handle; it must not be offered as an exhaustive technical definition of every schema.

**The current state and response:** what is happening now—hurt, fear, critical pressure, withdrawal, appeasing, or an adult response. “Mode” is clinical language for a current state/configuration, not a permanent identity.

The inherited script can contribute to the expectation, but not every schema comes from an explicit parental message. Nor does recognizing a current pattern prove its childhood origin or require locating a forgotten event. The diagram is a possible way to understand a pattern, not a universal developmental story. [Y, chs. 1 and 8; G, chs. 2–3.]

### What “a need” means here

The schema books’ common core-needs account includes safety and dependable care; autonomy, competence and identity; freedom to express feelings and needs; spontaneity and play; and realistic limits and self-control. These are explanatory families, not a questionnaire every reader must complete. Van Genderen’s newer presentation also includes fairness and self-coherence. Those themes already connect to the guide’s treatment of identity, meaning and justice; they are not grounds for inserting two more mandatory stages. Do not present one list as the final scientific inventory of human needs. [Y, ch. 1; G, ch. 2; H, ch. 4.]

The useful reader question is whether this particular moment calls for comfort, being heard, practical safety, room to choose, learning a skill, play, or a limit—not whether every difficult feeling needs the same soothing sentence. The three adult functions cooperate across these needs. A need for autonomy, for instance, can require the Nurturer to take a preference seriously, the Adult Protector to defend the person’s right to choose, and the Guide to help evaluate the choice. That is a mapping proposed for this guide, not a claim that the books assign one adult role to each need.

Some needs are met through relationships, resources or changes in circumstances. An internal conversation cannot substitute for everything missing outside. Choosing and arranging appropriate support can still be part of the person’s reparenting; the external help itself does not have to be relabelled an inner exercise.

### Exact placement and proposed content

In *The Parent You Inherited*, insert **after the second paragraph ending “of course your little one doesn't answer,” before “But with the borrowed adulthood trick…”** The existing third paragraph can then answer the expanded problem: the new adult is developed through care and action, not just a nicer internal argument.

Illustrative explanatory spine:

> Sometimes there isn’t a critical voice at all. You just expect that needing somebody will end badly, or feel ashamed before you can say why. The old rule is part of a bigger pattern: what you expect, remember and feel when something touches that place. Schema therapists call those enduring patterns schemas. You don’t have to find a forgotten scene or give the pattern another personality to notice how it affects you now.

Keep the current warmth/critic distinction in paragraph one. Do not turn “parent script” into an accusation against every parent, and do not imply that all body sensations reveal a schema. The guide’s *You Don’t Need an Inner Monologue* already protects nonverbal experience; this is a conceptual connection to it, not a second explanation of inner speech.

**Claude’s check:** Is this already explained elsewhere in sufficient form? If so, use a connection or reference instead. Does the passage overpromise that naming a schema explains the whole person? Does it preserve the difference between observed experience and a proposed interpretation?

## 4. C1 — Coping patterns: what the candy question revealed

### Why the earlier terminology fix was insufficient

Calling defensive reactions “guards” resolves a naming collision. It does not by itself explain how a behavior can repeatedly manage distress without presenting as a part that talks. Joel’s candy example exposed that additional explanatory need.

The current guide mentions sweets/screens under indulgence, a skeptical guard, urges to escape, and patterns of seeking reassurance. Those ingredients are present. What needs joining is **the behavior’s function**: what it changes immediately, what it avoids or seeks, and whether the person still attends to the underlying need afterward. This is a missing explanatory connection, not an assertion that the guide lacks behavioral work. [A, *The Inner Guide*, *Two Common Protective Patterns*, *When the Urge to Escape Arrives*, *When Healing Turns Into Checking*.]

### The three coping directions, with their limits

A small table or three connected examples can explain the older Schema Therapy terms without making them required vocabulary throughout the guide:

| Plain-language description | Older term | Example tied to a specific expectation |
|---|---|---|
| Live as though the painful expectation is simply true | Surrender | “My needs do not count,” so I consistently let others decide and do not state a preference |
| Avoid contact with the painful expectation or feeling | Avoidance | A rejection fear arises, and I repeatedly disappear into distraction instead of addressing what happened or what I need |
| Insist on or enact the opposite to keep the painful expectation away | Overcompensation | “I might be inadequate,” so I must appear flawless or superior and cannot tolerate an ordinary mistake |

These are possible functions, not labels inferred from a visible act. For example, agreeing with someone can express genuine agreement, a prudent safety choice, acceptance of a self-devaluing belief, or an attempt to prevent feared rejection. “People-pleasing” is not enough evidence to determine which. The proposed newer framework changes how some of these responses are grouped. [Y, ch. 1, pp. 34–42; G, ch. 2; R, pp. 1013–1015.]

Van Genderen draws on **resignation, avoidance and inversion**, following the 2021 theoretical reformulation. Do not present this as merely swapping words in an otherwise identical taxonomy or as a settled classification accepted by every practitioner. The reformulation emphasizes response to an activated internal schema, not a mechanical freeze/flight/fight mapping. Keep that detail in an appendix/source note unless the reader needs it. [R.]

### Candy: the same behavior, different possible jobs

| What is actually happening | What the guide should help the reader understand |
|---|---|
| I eat a sweet because I enjoy it | Enjoyment does not require a trauma explanation. There may be nothing to fix. |
| I deliberately have a comforting treat after a hard day, and still respond to my needs | Temporary soothing can be part of useful care. Relief is not automatically avoidance or failure. |
| I repeatedly turn to sweets or scrolling whenever hurt arises, feel unable to choose otherwise, and leave the real difficulty untouched | The pattern may be functioning as avoidant coping. Examine its benefit and costs, rather than condemning the food. |
| I insist on getting what I want immediately regardless of significant consequences or others’ boundaries | The response may call for limits and learning to tolerate frustration, whether or not distress is also present. |
| I loudly say “No” after always suppressing my preferences | This may be emerging protest or self-assertion, not selfish entitlement. The actual circumstances and behavior matter. |

This table is an **illustrative editorial synthesis**, not a diagnostic instrument or a claim that these five rows exhaust the possibilities. Several functions can coexist. A useful question is what the action is doing *in this instance*, not which internal character “owns” all candy eating.

The issue is not whether relief occurred, nor whether something was pleasurable. The useful distinction is whether the response is flexible and compatible with care, agency, actual constraints and other people’s rights—or repeatedly makes the person’s life smaller or more harmful. No moral score for eating candy is needed.

### Exact placement

Create one H3, **When a Habit Is Doing the Guarding**, **after the two existing paragraphs under Two Common Guarding Patterns and before Hear the Guard First**. Its opening should widen the examples just given: guarding sometimes appears as behavior rather than a voice. Keep the existing skeptical/escape examples and their uncertainty about yawning. This placement avoids interrupting the opening of *Start With Whatever Showed Up*, which already develops play, listening and uncertainty in a deliberate sequence.

C1, C2 and the main part of C3 belong **together in this one subsection**, not as three full new chapters. The full review here is longer than the public addition should be. A short example can carry the distinction, loop and adult response; a compact table is optional if prose handles it better.

Illustrative beginning:

> Sometimes a habit is doing the guarding. After a criticism you reach for sweets or your phone, and for a while the hurt is harder to feel. That doesn’t make sweets or distraction bad. The question is whether the break helps you come back to what needs care, or keeps replacing it. And sometimes you just want a sweet. There isn’t necessarily a frightened part hiding behind every desire.

The more technical three-style table can go in the appendix if it interrupts the guide’s voice. **Do not lose the broader coping explanation just because the clinical names are moved out of the main text.**

**Claude’s check:** Does the example accidentally moralize food, imply every soothing action is maladaptive, or confuse a child’s desire with defensive regulation? Is the book’s theory being attributed correctly rather than presented as a diagnosis of the reader?

## 5. C2 — Why the pattern can keep seeming true

### What this adds beyond “short-term relief”

Young describes schemas being maintained through selective interpretation of evidence, repeated life patterns and coping responses. Heath/Startup’s couples chapter describes cycles in which one person’s response becomes the other person’s trigger. These are explanatory models, not proof that any particular relationship problem is self-created. [Y, ch. 1, pp. 30–32; ch. 5; H, ch. 13.]

The guide already warns that a feeling does not prove its interpretation. The proposed addition shows **how interpretation and response can feed back into each other**.

Continue one everyday example rather than adding more characters:

> A friend hasn’t replied. You feel unimportant, disappear into your phone, and decide not to tell anybody you’re struggling. Later nobody offers support because nobody knows. That can look like fresh proof that nobody cares. Sometimes they really are unavailable; sometimes your way of protecting yourself has also kept them from seeing what you need.

This example is written for the review. It should not become a script telling a real person what caused their isolation. The alternative explanations matter: the friend may be busy, unreliable, unable to help, or genuinely uncaring. A one-off delay cannot settle those possibilities.

Another version is discounting care: somebody is kind, but the old expectation immediately explains it away as pity. That does not mean every compliment is sincere; it means the person can check the evidence without automatically discarding everything that contradicts the expectation.

### What must remain intact

The current guide explicitly rejects the claim that all problems are self-created. Keep that stance. Recognizing one’s contribution to a loop never transfers responsibility for another person’s cruelty, violence, deception or coercion. It does not require exposing vulnerability to unsafe people or testing whether a dangerous person might respond better.

A reciprocal loop can be useful to notice—one person presses for reassurance, the other withdraws, and each feels confirmed—but **mutual influence is not equal responsibility**. A coercive situation is not repaired simply by a nicer mutual communication exercise.

### Placement

Add the consequence-and-return portion **inside the same new H3 as C1**, after the example’s immediate relief and before the adult response in C3. This creates a complete sequence:

**Situation → felt expectation/meaning → reaction/coping → immediate effect → later consequence → revised response.**

That arrow sequence is an explanatory aid, not a claim that thoughts always precede feelings or that the person consciously notices each step. Experiences can overlap, and the person may recognize the pattern only afterward.

An optional single callback belongs in *Other Folks Also Have Hooks*, after the first paragraph’s question ending **“Or can they hear you disagree and look at their own part?”** It should distinguish repeating patterns from one isolated encounter. Do not add it if Claude finds the existing relational discussion already accomplishes the job.

**Claude’s check:** Does the loop explain more than the existing material without blaming the reader? Are hypothetical motives and effects clearly hypothetical? Does the example leave room for real harm and for changing the environment rather than merely changing perception?

## 6. C3 — What the adult does differently

### This is not a missing discipline section

*The Inner Guide* already combines care, direction and limits. *Not Every Hero Wears A Cape* already explains choosing an action, arranging resources, rehearsing communication, following through and interpreting the result. *Love Doesn’t Have to Wait for Trust* already teaches listening to a real complaint without capitulation or counterattack. The morning/evening practice already includes recognition, repair and appreciation. Do not write those lessons again.

The addition is **how to choose among these existing responses after understanding what the coping pattern does**. [A; source parallels Y, chs. 5–6; G, pp. 103–107, 178–200; H, chs. 4, 11, 14, 17.]

### Keep needs distinct from demands and strategies

A need for connection does not establish a right to a particular person’s immediate attention. A need for safety does not make every prediction of danger true. Wanting relief does not make every relieving behavior harmless. These are distinctions the guide already uses; the new coping explanation should lead back to them.

In the unanswered-message example, the Inner Adult might acknowledge the hurt, avoid deciding that silence proves rejection, assess whether the relationship is generally reliable, and choose an appropriate request or another source of company. That is one combined adult response, not a mandatory Nurturer-then-Protector-then-Guide ritual. If the relationship is actually unsafe, protection can be the immediate response. If resources are absent, do not invent a feasible solution by assuming them into existence.

For a mainly impulsive demand, warmth can remain while a limit stands. For legitimate protest, the task may be helping the person say no and take protective action. For an avoidant pattern, the task may be increasing tolerable contact with the difficulty and providing better support. They can overlap, so a rigid single-label prescription would defeat the purpose of this explanation.

### Caring about the guard does not require obeying it

The earlier guide already says to hear protective concerns without treating them as an oracle. Preserve that. A possible protective purpose deserves understanding; it is not proof that the proposed action is accurate, necessary or acceptable. The same applies when the person experiences self-criticism as protective.

The schema books also distinguish working with coping modes from confronting punitive or demanding internal messages. Do not flatten those into one universal instruction to “thank every critic and let it finish.” Nor should the guide adopt blanket banishment of every uncomfortable part merely because a book uses stronger externalizing language. [Y, ch. 8; G, pp. 164–169; H, chs. 9 and 11.]

The practical synthesis proposed here is: **interrupt humiliation, preserve any useful concern, and find a less harmful way to address it**. For example, “You never do anything right” can stop without ignoring an actual unpaid bill. The Guide can help open the envelope without adopting the contempt. The existing internal-repair and criticism material should remain the primary home; this is a connection, not another technique.

### Keep alternatives realistic

Do not demand that a person give up every short-term coping method before they have something workable to use instead. At the same time, this principle is not permission to continue a dangerous act until an ideal alternative appears. The guide’s immediate-safety boundaries remain. No abrupt medication or substance-withdrawal instruction belongs in this general habit paragraph.

### Placement and illustrative bridge

End the new coping H3 with a short bridge such as:

> Once you notice what the habit is doing, the question isn’t just how to stop it. What was it trying to get you through, and what would help now? Sometimes that’s comfort, sometimes a boundary, sometimes practice doing the difficult thing a little differently. Your Inner Adult can keep the concern and change the response.

Then let *Hear the Guard First* develop listening to a guard in its existing depth. In *The Inner Guide*, an optional short callback can follow the paragraph ending **“especially if getting your way would hurt you or somebody else.”** Its job is to distinguish a want from avoidant coping, not to retract the owner’s “No hidden protector or old trauma required” sentence.

**Claude’s check:** Is the proposed bridge already sufficiently present? Would a smaller cross-reference do? Has protection in daily life remained reparenting itself rather than a preliminary “external track” one must finish before the real work?

## 7. P1 — Prepare for a foreseeable difficult moment

### What is present and what might help

The daily-review practice already asks where the hook occurred, what happened, and what repair or protective action to bring. Van Genderen’s later chapter adds preparation for future triggers and a plan for renewed support when circumstances become difficult. [G, ch. 7, especially pp. 214–229.]

The proposed addition is modest: **on a steadier day, anticipate one likely situation, its early sign, and a first response**. This is different from constantly checking whether one is healed, compiling a large symptom diary, or requiring homework after each session.

An illustrative planning sentence is:

> If you already know tomorrow’s call tends to leave you saying yes to everything, decide beforehand what your first pause will be, and who you can check in with afterward.

The particular action should come from the person’s actual situation. “Pause” is an example, not a universal solution, and the plan must not require approaching an unsafe situation as an experiment. Add the principle that needing extra support during a setback does not erase the capacities already developed. Do not promise that a plan prevents relapse or guarantees calm.

### Exact placement

In *One Intention in the Morning, One Review at Night*, insert **after the quoted intention ending “I will notice a hook or 2 which lead to unconscious behaviors,” before “Then at night, pay yourself to be your own therapist…”** This section comes before the new coping explanation, so use plain language without relying on “schema,” “coping mode” or the new H3 as already introduced concepts.

Keep the playful intention, the evening questions, repair, appreciation and rest. If Claude finds the existing forward intention already adequate, this can be a one-sentence enrichment rather than a paragraph.

**Claude’s check:** Does it materially help the reader prepare, rather than merely restating the evening review? Does it avoid turning reparenting into continuous monitoring or a new performance requirement?

## 8. P2 — When a therapist or important support becomes less available

### What the books add

Heath/Startup’s chapter on endings treats separation as part of the therapeutic relationship, not merely an administrative stop. Van Genderen’s final phase discusses future help, endings and changes in the support friend’s role. [H, ch. 20; G, pp. 225–229.]

The current guide covers borrowed adulthood, distribution of support, grief and life transitions. It does not need another general essay on endings. The more specific useful connection is that **reducing help can bring up old fears even when real growth has occurred**, and that a person can retain learned care without pretending not to need people.

### Proposed teaching

Where feasible, discuss a foreseeable break or ending before it arrives: what has become the person’s own capacity, what still needs support, what can be carried in a brief reminder or remembered example, and what actually available help could be used later. A therapist or support person should not be assigned unlimited ongoing availability by the guide.

An ending does not automatically mean the relationship was fake. It also does not automatically prove successful graduation: some endings are abrupt, disappointing, constrained by money or circumstances, or responses to poor fit. The reader’s actual experience deserves room, rather than an imposed positive narrative.

Borrowed adulthood should not become permanent authority over the person’s life. But healthy development is not complete self-sufficiency either. The guide’s existing balance between inner and outer reliance stays intact.

### Exact placement

Prefer a short paragraph in *Finding a Therapist to Borrow Adulthood*, **after the paragraph ending “A certificate on the wall can't tell you any of that,” before “Regarding AI therapy…”** This lets a reader consider support boundaries and endings as part of choosing and working with a therapist. Point back briefly to *Borrowed Adulthood in Relationship* rather than repeat its function inventory.

Alternative only if Claude identifies a flow problem: place the personal-support aspect after the second paragraph of *Borrowed Adulthood in Relationship*, ending **“going too far in either extreme isn't generally the healthiest.”** Do not use both homes to explain the same lesson in full.

**Claude’s check:** Does the addition assume a good therapeutic relationship where none existed? Does it pathologize normal sadness, promise continuing contact, or equate independence with isolation?

## 9. F1 — Correct the Schema Therapy comparison without erasing the difference

### The exact claim that needs correction

The current appendix says:

> Where it really differs is spirituality. Schema therapy leaves it out.

Young, Klosko and Weishaar’s own preface, p. viii, explicitly discusses combining Schema Therapy with spirituality and names three books doing so. That is direct evidence against the categorical statement. It does **not** establish how frequently contemporary clinicians use spiritual material or that they use Joel’s particular understanding of divine love. [Y, preface p. viii.]

The defensible contrast is **emphasis and architecture**, not absolute inclusion versus exclusion. Joel’s guide makes spiritual love, gratitude, integration and the Guide function unusually explicit within his own synthesis. Schema Therapy has spiritual integrations but does not thereby share every one of those commitments.

### What else the comparison should preserve

The existing limited-reparenting paragraph is useful. It should acknowledge that the Healthy Adult includes guidance, practical judgment and limits as well as nurturing and protection. ## 10. F2 — What the Inner Brat means, and what not to add

### The correction to the earlier candy explanation

The original candy response contrasted the Inner Brat’s wish for gratification with a self-soothing response’s wish to escape distress. That contrast can be useful for two possible functions of behavior. It was **too narrow as an account of Capacchione’s whole Inner Brat discussion**.

Her chapter 4 includes self-centered wanting and the costs of letting it run one’s life. It also treats disowned angry wishes, rebellious expression and saying no as material that can be acknowledged rather than suppressed. She does not recommend letting the impulses have free rein. The book’s discussion therefore cannot be reduced either to “selfish indulgence” or to “healthy assertion.” [C, ch. 4, *The Inner Brat* and *Healing From the Inner Brat*, around printed pp. 107–111.]

Preserve the three distinctions already needed in C1: ordinary wanting; a protest or boundary that deserves a hearing; and conduct that ignores consequences or another person’s rights. Understand the feeling without automatically granting the demand or suppressing it as bad.

The current *The Inner Guide* already says that wanting can simply be wanting, without a hidden protector or trauma explanation. Preserve that. ## 11. O1 — Optional methods, not another compulsory stage
